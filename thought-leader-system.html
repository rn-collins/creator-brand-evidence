Why expert brands are different

A lawyer's LinkedIn post is not a founder's TikTok.

Generic personal-branding advice—post daily, be relatable, pick a niche—ignores that clinicians, lawyers, researchers, and educators are publishing under professional-responsibility rules a lifestyle creator never has to think about. An overstated claim, an implied result, or an untracked evidence source isn't just off-brand for an expert; it can be a licensing, malpractice, or credibility problem. This system treats content evidence rules and platform strategy as part of the same decision, not two separate workstreams.

Inside the deliverables

What four of these actually contain.

The list above is short because each item is a working document. Here is what the four that carry the most weight look like when they land.

Trust architecture

A one-page map of every signal a reader uses to decide whether to believe you: credentials and how they are stated, which claims carry a citation, how you handle corrections, what you disclose about commercial relationships, and where each of those appears across profile, post and long-form. Most expert brands have all of these and have never made them consistent.

Content evidence rules

A standing rule for each claim type you publish — what needs a primary source, what may be stated from experience and must be labelled as such, what needs a dated qualifier, and what is off-limits entirely. Written so a ghostwriter or an assistant can apply it without asking, which is the point at which the rules start saving time rather than costing it.

Editorial content pillars

Three to five recurring territories you can publish in for a year without repeating yourself, each with the audience it serves, the claims it is allowed to make, and the formats it suits. Pillars are chosen for what you can defend and sustain, not for what is performing on the platform this quarter.

Authority-building roadmap

A sequenced build rather than a posting calendar: which asset establishes the ground the later ones stand on, what gets published where, and in what order, so that a body of work accumulates. Sequencing is the difference between two years of posts and a reference people cite.

What a result looks like

Legible expertise, not a louder feed.

Illustratively: a solo attorney publishing case commentary might start with strong opinions and no system for what they can say about outcomes or clients. The trust architecture and evidence rules this system produces define what claims are safe to make, which platforms fit the audience they're actually trying to reach, and which editorial pillars build authority without drifting into advice a reader could reasonably rely on. The roadmap sequences that build so authority compounds instead of resetting with every new post.
